Azalea City

Valdosta, Georgia, is nicknamed the "Azalea City" and holds an annual Azalea Festival. The longer story starts with a parks man who was not trying to rebrand a town, and it still comes around each March in a park that bears his name.

Richard J. Drexel, an Indiana native, came to work for Valdosta in the early 1900s as, in the Lowndes County Historical Society's phrase, "Mr. Everything" for the city parks. In 1925 he became the city's director of parks and trees and began planting Formosa azaleas across town. His first job was to clean an overgrown park on college land. After the debris, the underbrush, and even a moonshine still came out, the azaleas went in. He later extended the plantings to the college campus, Sunset Hill Cemetery, and Patterson Street. Valdosta State University credits him with planting hundreds of thousands of Formosas, chosen because they were showy and easy to start from cuttings. He was not trying to rename the city. He just thought the bushes looked good. Local garden clubs suggested the "Azalea City" name in 1947. Drexel died in 1986, at 87. The nickname stuck because the plants did.

They had help from the climate. In south Georgia, the most popular azaleas are Southern Indian hybrids: large shrubs, often ten feet tall, that like the region's acidic soil, milder winters, and the filtered light under pine trees. Lowndes County Extension agent Jake Price lists familiar cultivars: 'Formosa,' with magenta-purple blooms; 'George Lindley Tabor,' white and purple; and 'Delaware Valley White.' Smaller Kurume hybrids, two to six feet tall, include 'Hinode Giri,' 'Coral Bells,' and 'Snow.' Native deciduous kinds also grow here, among them the Piedmont, Alabama, and Florida azaleas. The evergreen hybrids put on their main show in early spring, which is why the festival lands in mid-March, when the bushes are typically in bloom. Later natives can carry color into summer.

In 2000, Joanne Griner proposed a festival to celebrate that Azalea City status. She founded the Valdosta-Lowndes County Azalea Festival and stayed involved until her death in 2016. The first festival, on March 17, 2001, was a one-day gathering at Drexel Park that drew 8,000 to 10,000 people. It has been held there ever since, growing into a Saturday-and-Sunday event in mid-March. By 2017 it was drawing around 25,000 people and more than 200 vendors. It was canceled at the last minute in 2020 because of the pandemic, then came back. It is now a regional gathering, with more than 250 craft vendors, six stages, a classic car and motorcycle show, a KidZone, a 5K, and acts such as the Paul Bunyan Lumberjack Show. H. Aaron Strickland, board president since 2011, wanted it to be the largest festival of its kind within a two- or three-hour drive.

The nickname lasted because it described something people could see. In 2007 the Azalea Society of America made it official, certifying Valdosta as an Azalea City on March 10. Georgia had already named the azalea its state wildflower in 1979. Each March, the bushes and the festival still make the case Drexel made without meaning to: the place looks better in bloom, and the town is better for gathering around it.
